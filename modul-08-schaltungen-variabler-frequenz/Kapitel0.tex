\subsection{Frequency dependence of electrical components}
\begin{frame}\ftx{\subsecname}
    \renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
\s{% Skript-only
    Purely ohmic resistors $R$ cannot store electrical energy.
    Voltages and currents are proportional to each other and in phase at all times.
    
    This behaviour is independent of time and frequency.
    Inductances $L$ and capacitances $C$, on the other hand, can store and release energy.
    This process is inert (sluggish), meaning it takes a certain amount of time.
        
    As a result, their behaviour is frequency-dependent. 
    Currents and voltages in inductors and capacitors are in a (temporal) differential 
    linear relationship to each other. This results in a phase shift 
    between voltage and current of $90\ \degree$ for both types of components.
        
    Figure \ref{fig:bauteile:phasenverschiebung} shows the voltage and current curves for $R$, $L$ and $C$
    when excited with an alternating voltage $u_q = U \cdot \sin(\omega t)$ for comparison.

    Inductances and capacitances cannot therefore perform active power, only reactive power,
    which is why they are also called reactive resistances.
}

\subsubsection{Behaviour of inductances and capacitances}
\begin{frame}\ftx{\subsubsecname}
\s{% Skript-only
    Inductors, as ideal components, store energy in the magnetic field through the effect of (self-)induction.
    Capacitors, as ideal components, store energy in the electric field.
    Both effects are described by the law of induction and Gauss's law, respectively.
        
    Table \ref{tab:vergleich:induktivitaetkapazitaet} lists the most important differences 
    in the behaviour of inductors and capacitors in qualitative terms as an overview.

    \begin{table*}[h]\centering
        \renewcommand{\tablename}{Table}
    \caption{Comparison of inductance and capacitance in behaviour}
    \label{tab:vergleich:induktivitaetkapazitaet}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule&\textbf{Inductance} &\textbf{Capacity}\\
        \midrule
        \textbf{Law}                &Induction law          &Gauss's law            \vphantom{$\Big|$}\\
        \textbf{Energy storage}     &in the magnetic field  &in the electric field  \vphantom{$\Big|$}\\
        \textbf{continuous}         &current                &voltage                \vphantom{$\Big|$}\\
        \textbf{with DC voltage}    &short circuit          &open                   \vphantom{$\Big|$}\\
        \textbf{with high frequency}&open                   &short circuit          \vphantom{$\Big|$}\\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{table*}

    
    The (self-)inductance $L$ as a property can be simplified as \glqq inertia \grqq of the current. 
    Currents in inductors are continuous and lag behind the voltage.
    At high frequencies, the inductance blocks; at DC voltage, it behaves like a short circuit.
            
    In contrast, capacitance $C$ can be simplified as \glqq inertia \grqq of the voltage.
    Voltages in capacitors are constant and lag behind the current.
    With direct current, capacitance blocks the current; at high frequencies, it behaves like a short circuit.

    Figure \ref{fig:bauteile:realebauteile} shows real components that implement inductance and capacitance.
    Shown are a coil (inductance) and a capacitor (capacitance).
}
