	\ftb{Characterisation of the electric field}

	\s{As shown, electric charges have a direct influence on other charges.
    This occurs because they alter the properties of the space around them. These changes 
    can be described by a field model.
 	he concept of a field is of fundamental importance in physics. Examples
	of this are the temperature field, in which the temperature is described by a scalar quantity depending on its location.
    
	In addition to such scalar fields, there are also vector fields in which each location is assigned not only a quantity but also
    a direction in which it acts. Examples of this are the gravitational field or a
    flow field, which describes, for example, the direction and velocity of water particles.
    
	The \textbf{electric field} is described by \textbf{magnitude} (in newtons) and the \textbf{direction} 
    of the \textbf{force} $\vec{F_\mathrm{E}}$ on a positive test charge $Q_2$ and 
    characterised by field lines. This arrangement is shown in Figure \ref{fig:efeld_def}.

	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
	
		\includesvg[width=0.2\textwidth]{kraft_e}
		\caption{Attractive force $F_2$ of a test charge $Q_2$ in the direction of a stationary charge $Q_1$}
		\label{fig:efeld_def}
	\end{figure}


	The electric field strength is therefore:


	%\begin{equation*}
	\begin{Key point}{}
		$\mathrm{Electric \, field \, strength} = \frac{\mathrm{Force\, on\, load}}{\mathrm{Load}} $
	\end{Key point}	
	%\end{equation*}
\phantom{.}

	%\begin{equation}
	%	\vec{E} 
	%	
	%\end{equation}

	\begin{equation}
		\vec{E_1} = \frac{\vec{F_2}}{Q_2}  \quad \quad \left[ \frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}} =  \frac{\frac{\mathrm{kg \cdot m}}{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{A \cdot s}} = \frac{\mathrm{V}}{\mathrm{m}} \right]
		\label{eq:e_feld}
	\end{equation}


	At first glance, a disadvantage of this definition appears to be that the electric field strength seems to depend on the
    test charge $Q_2$. 
	However, by inserting Coulomb's law (equation \ref{eq:coulomb}) into equation \ref{eq:e_feld}, it can be shown 
    that the force $F_2$ always increases proportionally to the test charge $Q_2$. Their ratio therefore always yields the
    same value for the electric field strength, which, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:efeld1}, becomes independent of the test charge:


	\begin{equation}
		\vec{E_1} = \frac{\vec{F_2}}{Q_2} =\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{Q_1 \cdot Q_2}{r^2} \cdot \frac{1}{Q_2} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{Q_1}{r^2}
		\label{e_feld2}
	\end{equation}

		\includesvg[width=0.2\textwidth]{definition_e}
		\caption{The electric field caused by $Q_1$ is independent of any sample charge.}
		\label{fig:efeld1}
	\end{figure}

	\ftb{Electrical conductors and electrostatic fields I}

	\s{The previous chapter examines the effect of point charges on the electric field.
	However, since electric fields in everyday life often originate from charged electrical conductors (such as metals),
	they are listed separately here.
	If there is an excess of electrons, the positive test charge $Q$ is attracted towards the plate surfaces, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:leiter}.   
	is attracted towards the plate surfaces. The field lines $\vec{E}$ of the electric field also run in the direction of the 
    conductor surface. In the case of an electron deficiency, the test charge is repelled and the field lines also lead away from the positive conductor.


	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.5\textwidth]{leiter_pos_neg}
		\caption{Direction of the electric field strength $\vec{E}$ from a negatively (left) and positively (right) 
		charged conductor to a positive test charge}
		\label{fig:kraftwirkung}
	\end{figure}


	Since electrons are freely mobile within a conductor, in the case of an electric field within 
    a conductor, they would be attracted (or repelled) by this field until the field within the conductor 
    has balanced out (see Figure \ref{fig:leiterausgleich}). Consequently, in this case, no electric field can 
    exist within a conductor.

	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.5\textwidth]{Fehlvorstellung}
		\caption{Free charge carriers are instantly attracted to the edge of the conductor by the electric field. Therefore, no electrostatic field can exist within the conductor.
        electrostatic field.}
		\label{fig:leiterausgleich}
	\end{figure}

	This leads to the realisation that the electric field lines must necessarily be \textbf{perpendicular} to the
    surface of conductors (see Figure \ref{fig:e_vertikal}). Otherwise, an oblique field line could be vectorially
    decomposed into a component running perpendicular and a component running horizontally on the conductor surface. Since this 
    horizontal component is instantly balanced by the moving charge carriers, it cannot 
    exist. This effect occurs exclusively in electrically conductive materials; in non-conductive materials
    (so-called insulators), there are no moving charge carriers that can balance the horizontal component.


	 \begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.5\textwidth]{feldlinien_senkrecht}
		\caption{By eliminating the horizontal component $\vec{E_2}$ of the electric field, only the vertical component $\vec{E_1}$ remains, 
		which is perpendicular to the conductor surface.}
		\label{fig:e_vertikal}
	\end{figure}

	}

\subsection{Examples of electric fields}

\begin{frame}
	\ftx{Examples of electric fields I}

	\s{The simplest electric field is the \textbf{homogeneous field}. In this field, both
    the magnitude and the direction of the electric field strength $\vec{E}$ are constant at every point. A test charge located in this field
    therefore experiences the same force at every point.
    Such a field can be generated, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:homogenfeld}, for example between two parallel metal plates that are charged with different charges $Q$. 
	The field lines generally run from the 
    positively charged to the negatively charged metal plate. The density with which the
    field lines are drawn is often used as a measure of the strength of the electric field. 
    The denser the field lines are drawn, the stronger the electric field is at that point.

	  \begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.3\textwidth]{elektr_homogenfeld}
		\caption{An electric homogeneous field between two differently charged metal plates}
		\label{fig:homogenfeld}
	\end{figure}


	The electric field of a point charge is also referred to as the \textbf{radial field} (see Figure \ref{fig:punktladung}).
 	Under the idealised assumption that there is a shell with opposite charge infinitely far away,
 	the field lines propagate in a straight line.
	In their symmetrical arrangement, they point in the direction of the negative shell for positive point charges,
	and in the direction of the point charge for negative point charges. 

	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.26\textwidth]{feld_Punktladung}
		\caption{Radial electric field of a positive point charge. }
		\label{fig:punktladung}
	\end{figure}


	The electric field strength $\vec{E}$ is directly proportional to the charge $Q$, but decreases 
    quadratically with increasing distance $r$ from it. Its magnitude can be calculated using:


	\begin{equation}
				E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}
	\end{equation}


	The electrical field strength, which weakens with increasing distance $r$ from the point charge,
 	is evident not only in the mathematical relationship but also in the increasing distance between the field lines.


	More complicated electric fields arise when a second point charge is added.
	In the case of two charges of equal magnitude but opposite sign
	(Figure \ref{fig:anziehende_ladungen}), an almost uniform field exists between the charges, 
	while it becomes significantly weaker further out.
 

	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.44\textwidth]{feldlinien}
		\caption{The electric field of two charges of equal magnitude but opposite sign}
		\label{fig:anziehende_ladungen}
	\end{figure}

	The case with two identical point charges shows a special feature of the electric field, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:abstoßendeladungen}.
	While the field lines in the rest of the space point away from the positive charges, as expected,
	there is a free space between the point charges without any field lines.
	This is because the opposing field lines in this space between the charges cancel each other out. 
    There is no electric field strength at the point between the two charges; on the plane between the charges,
	the respective horizontal components neutralise each other, and there is only an electric field in the respective 
	vertical direction.

	
	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
		\centering
		\includesvg[width=0.4\textwidth]{feldlinien_protonen}
		\caption{The electric field of two charges of equal magnitude and the same sign}
		\label{fig:abstoßendeladungen}
	\end{figure}
