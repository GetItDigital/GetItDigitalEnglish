%Effektivwert

\begin{frame}
    \fta{RMS value} \label{Kap.Effektivwert}

    \s{
        Due to the periodic voltage curves, each time step has a different voltage value. 
        In this case, only the peak value can be specified directly, but this cannot always be used for calculations. 
        To ensure useful comparability of voltage values, 
        the effective value is used. 
        The RMS value describes, for example, the identical 
        power that would be generated at a resistor if it were connected to a direct current source.
        For a further explanation of the RMS value and the necessary fundamentals, this chapter discusses the following topics:
    }
    
    \begin{Learning objectives}{RMS value}
        The students
        \begin{itemize}
            \item understand the function of the effective value.
            \item are familiar with the effects of amplitude and waveform on the effective value.
            \item can calculate the effective value of an alternating voltage or alternating current. 
        \end{itemize}
    \end{Learning objectives}

\end{frame}

%Grundlagen
\begin{frame}
    \ftb{Fundamentals: Mean square and addition theorem}

    \s{
        In addition to the arithmetic mean, which is used in alternating current calculations to determine the mean value,
        the quadratic mean is used to determine the RMS value. The quadratic mean 
        (RMS – root mean square) is used to enable the comparability of alternating voltage values.      
        First, a function f(t) is squared. Then, the mean value of the squared function 
        is calculated. The final step is to take the square root of the calculated mean value. The mathematical relationship 
        of the RMS is shown in equation \ref{GleichungEff1}. 
    }

    \b{
        Der Effektivwert ermöglicht die Vergleichbarkeit von Wechselspannungen durch den quadratischen Mittelwert (RMS). 
        Der RMS wird über die Wurzel des Mittelwertes und die Quadratur einer Funktion ermittelt: 
    }

    \begin{eq}
        RMS=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^T f(t)^2 dt}          \label{GleichungEff1}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        When calculating the quadratic mean, the sine function is also squared. To assist with this, 
        an addition theorem is presented to simplify the calculation. Addition theorems
        refer to the relationships in trigonometry used to transform a function. The transformation of the 
        quadratic sine function using the addition theorem is shown in equation \ref{GleichungEff2}.
        Here, the quadratic sine function becomes a function that depends only on a simple cosine function.
    }

    \b{
        Bei der Berechnung des Effektivwertes ist das folgende Additionstheorem hilfreich:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        \sin^2(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos(2x))         \label{GleichungEff2}
    \end{eq}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \renewcommand{\figurename}{Figure}
    \ftb{Amplitude}

    \s{
        The amplitude of a function describes the maximum deflection during one period. For an alternating voltage, 
        the amplitude value indicates the highest instantaneous voltage value.
        In Figure \ref{BildAmplituden}, voltage signals with a frequency of $50\,Hz$ and thus with an identical period duration 
        of $T=20\,ms$ are shown. They differ in their amplitude values. The peak values of the amplitudes of the 
        voltages shown are $\hat{U}_1=1\,V$, $\hat{U}_2=2\,V$ and $\hat{U}_3=4\,V$.     
        The amplitude is a kind of factor for calculating the RMS value. Thus, the height of the RMS value depends directly
        on the amplitude value.
    }

    \b{
        Zusammenhang zwischen Amplitudenwert und Effektivwert:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Die Amplitude beschreibt den Scheitelwert $\hat{U}$ der Sinusspannung
            \item Verschiedene Amplituden bei unterschiedlichen Sinusspannungen
            \item Der Effektivwert ist direkt proportional zum Amplitudenwert $U_\mathrm{Eff} \sim \hat{U}$
        \end{itemize}
    }

\begin{frame}
    \ftb{Waveform} 

    \s{
        Neben der Amplitude eines Signals ist die Kurvenform des Signals ausschlaggebend für den Effektivwert. 
        Bei der Berechung des Effektivwerts wird der Scheitelfaktor $C$ (engl. für crest factor) bestimmt, welcher mit dem Ampltudenwert
        verrechnet wird. In der Gleichung \ref{GleichungKur1} wird die Beziehung zwischen dem Amplitudenwert und den 
        Effektivwert über den Scheitelfaktor angegeben.  
    }

    \b{
        Beziehung zwischen dem Scheitelwert und dem Effektivwert über den Scheitelfaktor:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        \hat{U} = C \cdot U_\mathrm{RMS}        \label{GleichungKur1}
    \end{eq}
       
    \s{
        This crest factor is $C_S=\sqrt{2}$ for sine waves, $C_D=\sqrt{3}$ for triangular waves,
        and $C_R=1$ for square waves. Thus, the RMS value of a sine wave voltage is
        approximately 70.7 per cent of the peak value of the amplitude.
        In addition to the crest factor as the relationship between the peak value and the effective value, 
        the form factor $F$ is also used. The form factor is defined as the quotient
        of the RMS value and the rectified value.

\begin{frame}
    \ftx{Einflüsse auf den Effektivwert}

    \begin{Key point}{Influences on the RMS value}
        The RMS value is linearly proportional to the peak value. The conversion factor from the RMS value to the peak value 
        is called the crest factor and depends on the waveform of the signal.
    \end{Key point}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
    \ftb{RMS value}

    \s{
        The RMS value, for example of a sinusoidal voltage, should be used for the power consumption as an instantaneous value 
        that causes the same power consumption that would correspond to the power value of a DC voltage under comparable 
        conditions.
         
        The RMS value is also applicable to Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws and is therefore
        suitable for all fundamental laws.
        The calculation of the RMS value will be explained using an alternating current signal $i(t)$. If the definition 
        of the current signal is inserted into equation \ref{GleichungEff3}, the result is:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        f(t) = i(t) = \hat{I} \cdot \sin(\omega t) \quad \rightarrow \quad I_\mathrm{Eff}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^T {\hat{I}}^2 \cdot \sin^2(\omega t) dt}    \label{GleichungEff3}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        The square root of the peak value is not time-dependent and can be moved outside the integral sign. In addition, 
        the factor from the addition theorem can be removed from the integral, resulting in the following equation \ref{GleichungEff4}:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        I_\mathrm{RMS}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{I}^2}{2T}\int_{0}^T {1-\cos(2\omega t) dt}}      \label{GleichungEff4}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        According to the difference rule, differences in an integrand can be determined separately. In this way
        in the following equation \ref{GleichungEff5}, the integrals for the two operands of the difference are determined separately. 
    }

    \begin{eq}
        \text{with} \quad \int_0^T 1 dt = T \quad \text{and} \quad \int_0^T \cos(2 \omega t) dt = 0        \label{GleichungEff5}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        Solving the integral yields:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        I_\mathrm{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{I}^2}{2T}\cdot (T - 0)}          \label{GleichungEff6}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        Here, the period duration $T$ can be reduced from the numerator and denominator, resulting in the following relationship:
    }

    \begin{eq}
        I_\mathrm{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{I}^2}{2}}              \label{GleichungEff7}
    \end{eq}

    \s{
        Now the root is taken separately from the numerator and denominator. The same relationship can also be defined 
        for the voltage:

    \begin{eq}
        I_\mathrm{RMS} = \frac{\hat{I}}{\sqrt{2}}   \qquad \text{and}   \qquad      U_\mathrm{RMS} = \frac{\hat{U}}{\sqrt{2}}        \label{GleichungEff8}
    \end{eq}
    }

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
    \ftx{Effektivwert einer Sinusschwingung}

    \begin{Key point}{RMS value of a sinusoidal oscillation}
        For purely sinusoidal voltages and currents, the crest factor $\sqrt{2}$ applies:
            \begin{eq}
            I_\mathrm{RMS} = \frac{\hat{I}}{\sqrt{2}}   \qquad \text{and}   \qquad      U_\mathrm{RMS} = \frac{\hat{U}}{\sqrt{2}} \nonumber
        \end{eq}
    \end{Key point}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \ftx{Beispielaufgabe}
	\begin{expl}{RMS value}{Effektivwert:}

    \s{
        If the voltage is measured at a domestic socket, for example, the RMS value of 230V is specified. 
    }

    \begin{enumerate}
        \only<1>{	
            \item[]
            
            The following tasks should be completed to gain a better understanding of the RMS value: 
                \begin{itemize}
                \item[a)] Calculation of the amplitude value of the voltage at a domestic power socket.
                \item[b)] Calculation of the RMS value assuming that it is a rectangular signal.
            \end{itemize}
        }

        \only<2>{	
            \item[a)]
            The amplitude value at a socket with an RMS value of 230V is: 
                \begin{eqa}
                \hat{U} &= I_\mathrm{RMS} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 230V \cdot \sqrt{2}      \nonumber   \\
                \hat{U} &= 325,269V         \nonumber
            \end{eqa}  
        }

        \only<3>{	
            \item[b)]
            The RMS value of a square wave voltage with a peak value of 325V is: 
                \begin{eqa}
                U_\mathrm{Rectangle} &= \frac{\hat{U}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{325V}{\sqrt{3}}      \nonumber   \\
                U_\mathrm{Rectangle} &= 187,639V         \nonumber
            \end{eqa}  
        }

    \end{enumerate}
    \end{expl}
\end{frame}
